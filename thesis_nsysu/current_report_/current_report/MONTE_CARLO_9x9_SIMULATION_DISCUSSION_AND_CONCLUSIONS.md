# Monte Carlo 9×9 Antenna Simulation: Discussion and Conclusions

## Technical summary

The two notebooks analyzed here compare a 38 GHz indoor channel in a 20 m × 20 m × 3 m room using two receiver configurations:

1. **Without a lens**, using the same patch-antenna pattern for every RX configuration.
2. **With a lens**, using different HFSS far-field patterns for lens angles of +60°, +45°, +30°, +15°, 0°, −15°, −30°, −45°, and −60°.

Each notebook performs 20 Monte Carlo drops. Every drop contains nine randomly positioned transmitters, and the same TX positions are tested against all nine RX configurations. Each notebook therefore contains 180 drop–RX results. This paired design provides a fairer comparison between lens angles and between the lens and no-lens cases because each pair uses identical TX geometry.

The aggregate results show that the lens increases median capacity from **7.29 to 7.67 bit/s/Hz** and median ideal throughput from **2.92 to 3.07 Gbit/s**. However, the benefit is not uniform across all directions. Intermediate negative angles—particularly **−30°**—produce the best median performance, while **+60°** performs worse than the no-lens antenna. Because the simulation uses only 20 drops and 100,000 path samples per source, these results should be treated as preliminary rather than final.

## Simulation sources

- `Setup_20mx20m_wolens_9x9_Patch_randomCom.ipynb`: the no-lens RX scenario, using `FarFieldData_Patch_38GHz.csv` for all angle labels.
- `Setup_20mx20m_lens_9x9_Patch_randomCom.ipynb`: the lens-equipped RX scenario, using HFSS far-field patterns associated with the configured angles.

The figures reported in this document come from outputs already stored in the two notebooks. The notebooks were not re-executed because this discussion evaluates the Monte Carlo results that had already been generated.

## Calculation methodology

### Channel generation

For each drop, nine TXs are placed at random positions and pointed toward the RX. All RX configurations are then tested using the same set of TX positions. The wideband channel response is calculated at 401 frequency points.

Although the notebook filenames contain the word `Patch`, the Monte Carlo section explicitly forces the TX pattern to be **isotropic (`iso`)**. Channel variation in the random-position experiment is therefore primarily caused by TX position, room propagation, and the RX pattern—not by TX patch directivity.

### Channel metrics

Power from the nine TXs is combined non-coherently:

$$
P_\mathrm{RX}(f)=P_\mathrm{TX}\sum_{i=1}^{9}|H_i(f)|^2.
$$

Noise is calculated using $kTB$ and the configured noise figure. The equivalent SISO spectral efficiency is:

$$
C=\mathbb{E}_f\left[\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR}(f))\right].
$$

Throughput is the ideal value $B\times C$, not actual protocol throughput. RMS delay spread is obtained from the power-delay profile generated by applying an IFFT to the channel response.

### Virtual MIMO evaluation

The nine RX channels associated with different angles are combined into a “9×9 MIMO” matrix to calculate condition number, effective rank, and capacity at an SNR of 10 dB. These nine RX measurements do not occur simultaneously at one physical receiver. The result is therefore suitable as an indicator of virtual channel diversity or conditioning, but it must not be interpreted as capacity directly achievable by a physical single-antenna RX.

## Aggregate lens and no-lens results

| Metric | Without lens | With lens | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Median capacity | 7.29 bit/s/Hz | 7.67 bit/s/Hz | +0.38 bit/s/Hz (+5.2%) |
| 5th-percentile capacity | 6.48 bit/s/Hz | 5.02 bit/s/Hz | −1.46 bit/s/Hz |
| 95th-percentile capacity | 9.21 bit/s/Hz | 9.68 bit/s/Hz | +0.47 bit/s/Hz |
| Median ideal throughput | 2.92 Gbit/s | 3.07 Gbit/s | +0.15 Gbit/s (+5.1%) |
| Median RMS delay spread | 68.29 ns | 65.01 ns | −3.28 ns (−4.8%) |
| Capacity outage below 1 bit/s/Hz | 0.0% | 0.0% | No change |

The lens improves typical performance and the upper end of the distribution, but it degrades the 5th percentile. The capacity range is also much wider with the lens. This indicates that the lens provides greater gain when its direction is favorable, while introducing increased sensitivity to angle and configuration. The no-lens antenna produces more uniform performance.

The 1 bit/s/Hz outage threshold is too low to distinguish the two configurations in this sample because all 360 results remain above it. Additional thresholds—such as 5, 6, or 7 bit/s/Hz—should be reported to evaluate reliability more meaningfully.

## Comparison by RX angle

| Angle | Median capacity without lens | Median capacity with lens | Median difference |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| +60° | 7.31 | 6.70 | −0.61 |
| +45° | 7.31 | 7.49 | +0.18 |
| +30° | 7.31 | 7.29 | −0.01 |
| +15° | 7.30 | 8.02 | +0.72 |
| 0° | 7.30 | 7.68 | +0.39 |
| −15° | 7.29 | 8.28 | +0.99 |
| −30° | 7.29 | **8.32** | **+1.03** |
| −45° | 7.29 | 8.13 | +0.84 |
| −60° | 7.29 | 7.43 | +0.14 |

Capacity values in the table are expressed in bit/s/Hz.

Without the lens, median capacity across all angle labels varies by only approximately 0.02 bit/s/Hz. This is expected because every configuration uses the same patch RX pattern and the RX positions differ by only a few centimeters. With the lens, median capacity ranges from 6.70 to 8.32 bit/s/Hz, making the RX angular pattern a major factor.

The best performance occurs at −30°, followed by −15° and −45°. In contrast, +60° is the weakest configuration. The asymmetry between positive and negative angles means that the system cannot be assumed to be symmetric based only on the magnitude of the angle. The difference may be caused by the far-field patterns, RX orientation, HFSS data mapping, parameter filtering, or interaction between the antenna pattern and room geometry.

## Paired checks by drop

The output log for every drop was used to calculate “with lens minus without lens” differences across 180 pairs with identical TX positions. Because values in the log are rounded, the following results should be treated as checks of the effect direction rather than full-precision estimates:

- median capacity difference: **+0.40 bit/s/Hz**;
- mean capacity difference: **+0.15 bit/s/Hz**;
- the lens produces higher capacity in **106 of 180 pairs (58.9%)**;
- median SNR difference: **+1.3 dB**;
- median RMS-delay-spread difference: **−4.70 ns**.

By angle, the lens performs better in 15 of 20 drops at +15° and −15°, 14 of 20 drops at 0° and −30°, and 13 of 20 drops at −45°. At +60°, the lens performs better in only 8 of 20 drops. These paired results strengthen the conclusion that lens benefits depend on angular alignment and do not constitute a universal improvement.

## Virtual MIMO channel conditioning

| Metric | Without lens | With lens |
|---|---:|---:|
| Median condition number | 49.4 (33.8 dB) | 54.0 (34.6 dB) |
| 5th–95th percentile condition-number range | 27.8–44.4 dB | 29.4–40.6 dB |
| Median effective rank | 4.09 of 9 | 4.07 of 9 |
| Median capacity at 10 dB | 21.72 bit/s/Hz | 21.11 bit/s/Hz |

The lens does not improve virtual MIMO conditioning in this configuration. The median condition number increases slightly, effective rank is almost unchanged, and median capacity at 10 dB decreases by approximately 0.61 bit/s/Hz. An effective rank close to four also indicates that the nine angular channels do not provide nine independent spatial modes.

This does not conflict with the increase in aggregate SISO capacity. A lens can increase power or SNR at selected angles without improving channel orthogonality.

## Limitations and uncertainty

1. **The number of drops is small.** Twenty drops are sufficient for an initial test but insufficient for stable estimation of distribution tails and outage probability.
2. **Path sampling is not set to the recommended final value.** The notebooks recommend 1,000,000 samples per source, whereas the stored results use 100,000.
3. **The Monte Carlo TX pattern is isotropic.** The results do not measure TX patch-pattern effects even though the notebook filenames contain `Patch`.
4. **Total power increases with the number of TXs.** Every TX uses 10 dBm. If the objective is comparison under fixed total system power, per-TX power should be reduced by $10\log_{10}(9)\approx9.54$ dB.
5. **Capacity is an ideal Shannon bound.** The values do not include discrete modulation, practical coding, overhead, interference, channel estimation, or RF implementation losses.
6. **No-lens angle labels do not represent different angular patterns.** Every no-lens scenario uses the same patch CSV; the labels primarily provide matching identifiers for the lens configurations.
7. **The lens configurations are asymmetric.** RX orientation and HFSS parameter filtering differ for some angles. Positive/negative angle mapping should be verified before the observed asymmetry is treated as a physical result.
8. **The 9×9 MIMO model is virtual.** The nine RX states are not measured simultaneously, so the reported MIMO capacity is not directly realizable by a single physical RX.
9. **The notebook outputs contain an environment warning.** An LLVM JIT initialization failure appears near the start of the notebooks, although the stored Monte Carlo outputs are complete. Execution stability and backend consistency should be checked during reproduction.

Given these limitations, the most appropriate status is **share with caveats**, rather than treating the analysis as a final general result.

## Recommended follow-up simulations

1. Increase the number of drops to at least 100–500 and use 1,000,000 path samples per source.
2. Export the complete results table to CSV or Parquet so statistical tests do not depend on rounded logs.
3. Report bootstrap confidence intervals for paired capacity differences at every angle.
4. Add more demanding outage thresholds, such as 5, 6, and 7 bit/s/Hz.
5. Repeat the experiment with a fixed total power budget to separate antenna benefits from the increased total power of nine TXs.
6. Run two separate TX configurations: isotropic patterns to isolate position effects and HFSS patch patterns for a more realistic device representation.
7. Verify boresight direction, angle signs, RX orientation, ±45° data filters, and positive/negative HFSS-file mapping.
8. If physical MIMO capacity is the objective, model multiple simultaneously active RX elements with an appropriate power-allocation and noise model.

## Conclusion

The Monte Carlo simulation indicates that using a lens at the RX provides a **moderate improvement in typical performance**, increasing median capacity and ideal throughput by approximately 5%. The largest benefits occur from −15° to −45°, with −30° producing the best result. The lens also slightly reduces median RMS delay spread.

However, the lens **does not improve every channel condition**. Low-percentile performance degrades, +60° performs worse than the no-lens case, and the virtual MIMO metrics do not show improved conditioning. In other words, the lens behaves as a direction-selective gain mechanism: it is beneficial when the angle and pattern are aligned but can be detrimental under angular mismatch.

The principal conclusion from the two notebooks is that the lens has the potential to improve system capacity, but its success depends on **angle selection, orientation, and far-field-pattern mapping**. Before the findings are used as the basis of a final design, the simulation should be expanded, run with final path-sampling settings, compared under a fair total-power budget, and validated against a physically realizable antenna configuration.
